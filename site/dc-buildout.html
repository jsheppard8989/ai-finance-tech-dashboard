The AI Data Center Buildout After the October Selloff

Who gets paid when the industry spends hundreds of billions on AI data centers, and who carries the risk if it overbuilds.

Paper only: no real trades or orders

Research desk, Oct 18, 2026. Prices are Friday Oct 16 closing prices unless a date is given. Times are Central (CT).

In plain English

Will the demand be there? Yes. The demand is real, and everyday use of AI is the strongest argument against a glut (more data center capacity than paying customers). This selloff is about who pays for the buildout: a glut is more a financing question than a physics question.

The ranking: Strong: CIFR, APLD, WULF. Middle: CORZ, NBIS, IREN, GLXY, ORCL. Weak: CRWV. Five are borderline (CIFR, APLD, CORZ, WULF, NBIS), and WULF's tier is provisional. Details in section 5.

The Meta trigger: When Meta reports on Wed Oct 21, we check first for a spending cut, then for a big 2027 raise. A cut means a half-size paper trade that profits if CoreWeave falls, a raise means one that profits if Nebius rises, and anything else (the most likely case) means no trade.

CORZ desk call: long CORZ on a trigger only, no short, now with a lower entry cap ($12.85) and a stop on a daily close 1.5 ATR below entry. It probably won't fill. CORZ owns the power and the buildings CoreWeave rents (and CoreWeave tried to buy it in 2025); a dissenting reviewer's (pen name John Galt) long CRWV / short CORZ pair is the labeled dissent.

In plain words: "long" means owning the stock to profit if it rises; "short" means selling borrowed shares to profit if it falls. ATR (average true range) is the stock's average daily price range, here $1.11 over the last 14 trading days, so 1.5 ATR is $1.67. Details in section 9.

What we're doing now: no new position before the big cloud companies (Microsoft, Alphabet/Google, Amazon, Meta) report on Oct 28–29 and CoreWeave files its next quarterly report (due by Nov 9). Everything on this page is a paper exercise.

Companies (ticker): CoreWeave (CRWV), Core Scientific (CORZ), TeraWulf (WULF), Applied Digital (APLD), Galaxy Digital (GLXY), Cipher Digital, formerly Cipher Mining (CIFR), IREN (IREN), Nebius Group (NBIS), Oracle (ORCL). Is the usage real?

  • Google processes "over 3.2 quadrillion" tokens a month (tokens are the chunks of text an AI model reads and writes), seven times a year earlier. Source: Google I/O, May 2026
  • ChatGPT has 1.2 billion weekly users, per OpenAI. Source: The Verge, Sep 29
  • NVIDIA's sales to AI start-ups, businesses and governments grew 138% from a year earlier, against 102% for the big cloud companies. Source: NVIDIA CFO commentary
  • Anthropic has reportedly committed at least $518B to computing over about 10 years, about 80% of it non-cancelable (section 11). Source: Reuters, Sep 29, citing Anthropic's confidential filing to list its shares (IPO)

Our view: the weak spot is demand that depends on suppliers lending to their own customers, and on AI labs signing commitments far larger than their revenue.

2. Supply, and the odds of a glut

  • NVIDIA's data center revenue was $89.0B in its May–Jul quarter, up 117% from a year earlier. It forecast $108.0B of total revenue next quarter. Source: NVIDIA release
  • TSMC, which makes the chips, called AI chip demand "extremely robust" on Oct 15. Source: TSMC 3Q26 call
  • Two chip parts are still short: advanced packaging (the step that joins AI chips to their memory) and high-bandwidth memory (fast memory stacked next to the chip). An analyst estimate has packaging still 15–20% short at the end of 2027, and Micron says about 75% of its 2027 high-bandwidth memory output is committed. Sources: KGI, Sep 15; TNW

What a gigawatt costs. NVIDIA's CEO Jensen Huang put the all-in cost of a gigawatt of AI data center at $50–60B, of which NVIDIA's share is about $35B: "Out of a gigawatt AI factory, which can go anywhere from $50 billion to, plus or minus 10%, let's say, $50 billion to $60 billion, we represent about $35 billion, plus or minus, of that." That's a vendor's own estimate (reported). Source: NVIDIA earnings call transcript, Aug 27, 2025

Our research lead's estimate: dividing AI spending by that cost, the spending already paid for buys about 8.3–10.0 GW in 2026 and 10.3–12.4 GW in 2027. Chip supply is about 10–15 GW a year now, and 17–25 GW a year in 2027–28 if chip output and its parts allow. So 2026 roughly matches. In 2027–28, chip supply could grow faster than paying demand unless spending beats the +28% forecast, or AI labs fill the gap with money from stock-market listings (IPOs) and loans from their suppliers. A lower cost per gigawatt would raise paying demand and narrow the 2027 gap.

3. Power is the bottleneck, and it's local

The limit is substations, grid connections and utility approvals, not total US power capacity. A typical project built in 2024 took 55 months from its request to connect to the grid until it started running, up from 36 months in 2015. Source: Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, Queued Up 2025

  • PJM, the grid operator for much of the eastern US, held its 2028/29 capacity auction (where power plants are paid to be available). The price cleared at the maximum allowed, $325 per MW per day, and the auction still came up 6,831 MW short of its reliability target. The next auction opens Dec 9, 2026. Source: PJM
  • PJM plans an emergency power-buying filing with federal regulators on Thu Oct 29, with a members' meeting on Oct 22. The cost would go to the areas adding big new loads like data centers. Source: PJM presentation
  • Virginia's utility regulator, the State Corporation Commission (SCC), ordered Dominion Energy Virginia on Jul 31, 2026 to file, "within 90 days," a revised policy that makes large users such as data centers pay up front for "direct connect" transmission lines (a "contribution in aid of construction"). 90 days from Jul 31 is Thu Oct 29, 2026 (our date arithmetic). Source: SCC Final Order, Case No. PUR-2026-00056
  • The federal power regulator (FERC) ordered all six grid operators on Jun 18, 2026 to show how they will connect large loads such as data centers (section 206 show-cause orders). For PJM, an Aug 14, 2026 order (Docket No. EL26-67-000) paused the case and set responses due Mon Nov 16, 2026, with PJM's own filing due by then. Sources: FERC news release, Jun 18, 2026; FERC order, Aug 14, 2026, as posted by PJM
  • Cipher Digital's Black Pearl site in Texas delivered its first data center capacity, with rent started, in August 2026: "First data center capacity at Black Pearl delivered in August, with rent commenced." That was two months ahead of the original October 2026 schedule, under a lease change the tenant asked for. Applied Digital's Harwood, North Dakota campus faced local opposition. Sources: TeraWulf release; Lexington Herald-Leader; North Dakota Monitor

Our view: Oct 28–29 is when everything lands at once. The big cloud companies report, PJM files its power plan, and Dominion's Virginia policy is due.

4. Who gets paid first

Our ranking of who collects first when money flows through the buildout:

  1. Owners of firm grid power and grid connections
  2. NVIDIA, and lenders whose loans have GPUs (the AI chips) as collateral
  3. Landlords whose rent is backed by a strong tenant or a guarantee
  4. Bondholders with no collateral, including holders of convertible bonds (bonds that can turn into shares)
  5. Shareholders of companies that own GPUs

The market already ranks names this way. From Oct 9 to Oct 16, Core Scientific (a landlord) fell 12.5% and CoreWeave (a GPU owner) fell 25.2%. So the ranking by itself isn't a trade. It tells you where to stand when results come out. Source: Yahoo Finance daily closes

5. Rankings: nine companies, one cash test

Every company gets the same test: how many quarters its spendable cash lasts at its recent rate of spending. That's its runway, and the spending rate is its burn (cash going out each quarter, after the business's own cash coming in). 6 quarters or more, or no burn, is good; under 4 is a warning. A company is borderline if one judgment call, one missing number or one reserve would flip its tier, if the two views disagree, or if runway sits within about 10% of a cut-off. It's provisional if the tier rests on an estimate that can't be computed from what's filed. The full rule is under "Show the math" below the table.

Show the math

The runway rule

  • What counts as cash: only money the company can spend on anything. That's unrestricted cash plus unused general-purpose credit lines at the latest quarter end. Cash locked up for a project, held in escrow or set aside to pay lenders doesn't count, and neither do loans that can only pay for one named build.
  • What counts as spending: operating cash flow minus capital spending. Builds paid for by their own project loans are taken out, because the loans and the builds cancel out. That holds whether the loan money sits in a locked account or in ordinary cash. If nothing is left burning, the company has no corporate burn. Spending is cash actually paid, not spending committed for later. That favors companies with big unfunded commitments (Oracle's leases, Cipher Digital's Barber Lake, TeraWulf's new halls). We note it but don't adjust for it.
  • Money raised after the quarter ended isn't counted for anyone. All nine companies were searched the same way through Oct 18, and those raises are listed below.
  • The cut-offs: 6 quarters or more, or no corporate burn, gets the higher of two separate tier calls by our analysts. 4 to under 6 quarters gets MIDDLE. Under 4 gets the lower tier, capped at MIDDLE: if both calls say STRONG but runway is under 4, the tier is MIDDLE.
  • The all-in check counts everything instead, including locked-up cash and project-only loans.

Balance sheets are at the latest quarter end each company has filed (Jun 30, 2026 for most; Aug 31 for Applied Digital and Oracle). Oracle's runway counts its $10.0B revolver. Whether any of it was drawn at Aug 31 is NOT FOUND; it's taken as undrawn, which can't change the tier. Nebius has no revolver. CoreWeave's and IREN's filings don't split loan draws by facility, so their figures are bounds ("at least"), but the tier holds either way.

6. Who depends on whom

  • CoreWeave's top three customers were 36%, 26% and 10% of Q2 revenue. A year earlier one customer was 71%. Source: CoreWeave quarterly report
  • CoreWeave was about 77% of Core Scientific's first-half revenue. Source: Core Scientific quarterly report
  • CoreWeave is the only AI tenant at Galaxy Digital's Helios campus, and Applied Digital's first North Dakota campus is leased to CoreWeave. Cipher Digital's tenants are Amazon Web Services and Fluidstack (backed by Google), not CoreWeave. Sources: company filings; Cipher Digital presentation, Feb 24, 2026

So CoreWeave is spreading its customers out while its landlords aren't. That's why Core Scientific stays MIDDLE, and why Applied Digital's STRONG carries a CoreWeave-tenant flag.

7. The commodity: chip rents vs how long chips are assumed to last

  • CoreWeave and Oracle spread the cost of their servers over six years, and Meta over 5.5 years. Sources: CoreWeave 10-K, Oracle 10-Q, Meta 10-K
  • Microsoft's "useful life update," in its CFO's words: "effective at the start of FY27, we are extending the estimated useful lives of our datacenters and office buildings, from 15 to 25 years," with "a minimal benefit to FY27 operating income," and "more of our future datacenter leases will shift from finance leases to operating leases." Microsoft didn't describe a change to server or GPU lives on that call. Source: Microsoft FY26 Q4 call (CFO Amy Hood), Jul 29, 2026
  • Prices on Kalshi, a prediction market, put the rent for an NVIDIA H100 chip at $2.90 an hour for Oct 2026, falling to $2.27 by Jun 2027 (as of Oct 14, 2026), about 22% lower. Little trades there. Data: AI Compute Tracker, from Kalshi, licensed CC BY 4.0

Our view: a six-year life on hardware whose rent is expected to fall that fast flatters the reported profits of chip owners. If a glut comes, that's how it starts. It hits chip owners and their lenders without collateral first, and landlords last.

8. CoreWeave's bonds: our call

The one place the market looks too gloomy is CoreWeave's 9.625% bonds due 2032. Bond prices from FINRA TRACE (the public record of US corporate bond trades that dealers must report; CUSIP 21873SAK4): on Oct 15 the bonds traded between 75.00 and 82.50 cents on the dollar, mostly around 78 (median 78.00 across 106 trades), and the last trade that day was 81.72 (FINRA bond page; trade history). Press coverage at the time matched (Morningstar/PitchBook, Oct 16). Reported press quote, not exchange data: the price of five-year default insurance on CoreWeave (credit default swaps, or CDS) hit a record of about 1,194.5 basis points (hundredths of a percentage point) that week (Startup Fortune citing Bloomberg, Oct 16). We had no dealer quotes or official closing prices for the default insurance. Our estimate is that those levels imply roughly 56–62% odds of default by 2032, against about 41% at our own odds.

But a lot of debt ranks ahead of these bonds, which have no collateral behind them. If CoreWeave defaulted, these bondholders would probably get back much less than the usual 30–40 cents on the dollar. At our 15-cent estimate, the bonds are only about 7% cheap, which isn't enough. So we dropped the plan to buy at 80–82.

Why not CoreWeave stock? At our odds its expected return (the probability-weighted average outcome) is about −5.3%, and it ranks last, behind all of the debt. The landlords' senior bonds (first in line for repayment) trade near full value (Cipher Digital's 2030s at 99.875, reported press quote), so there's nothing left to buy there.

9. Core Scientific: we lean long, on conditions only

"Long" means owning the stock to profit if it rises; "short" means selling borrowed shares to profit if it falls.

What decides it

  • About 590 MW across five sites is 100% licensed to CoreWeave. Initial terms are 12 years (7 at Austin), with fees rising 3.5% a year. CoreWeave pays the construction costs, and the contracts total about $10B of revenue. Source: Core Scientific filing, Apr 21, 2026
  • The contract summary says CoreWeave has "no ability to unilaterally terminate." Source: Core Scientific presentation, May 6, 2026
  • No outside guarantee stands behind CoreWeave's rent. Core Scientific says its ability to collect "would depend on [CoreWeave's] financial condition at the time of any default." Source: Core Scientific quarterly report
  • CoreWeave agreed to buy Core Scientific in July 2025. Core Scientific's shareholders voted it down on Oct 30, 2025, with 203.5M votes against and 20.8M for. Source: Core Scientific filing, Oct 31, 2025 The tenant tried to own its landlord's power and buildings, which shows the switched-on site, not the chip, is the scarce piece.
  • Core Scientific signed AMD for 377 MW and another AI cloud provider for 152 MW, each for 15 years. It has to pay to build those sites itself. Source: Core Scientific quarterly report (above)

If CoreWeave fails

Core Scientific loses the tenant, not the asset. It keeps the power and the buildings and can re-lease them, though at lower rents in a glut, and its secured bondholders (those with collateral) get paid first on the CoreWeave sites. In that same case CoreWeave's stock goes to near zero.

Three risks in Core Scientific's own filings

  • Warrants already in the money. A warrant is a right to buy new shares from the company at a set price. Core Scientific has 96.4M warrants that let holders buy at $6.81, far below the $13.48 Oct 16 close, expiring Jan 23, 2027, plus 6.6M more at $0.01. Holders must act by Jan 23, 2027, so up to 96.4M new shares could come to market during or just after the trade window. Source: Core Scientific 10-Q, Q2 2026, Note 8
  • Convertible notes also in the money. A convertible note is a bond the holder can swap for shares at a set price. Its $460M of 3.00% notes due 2029 convert at about $11.00 a share, below today's price, and the filing says they "remain convertible during the third quarter of 2026." That's about 41.8M shares. Its $625M of notes due 2031 (about $22.49 a share) and AMD's warrant ($23.47) are both above the $15.41 target, so we leave them out. Source: Core Scientific 10-Q, Q2 2026, Note 7
  • A credit line that can vanish when it's needed. The $100M revolver has a market-cap condition: Core Scientific can borrow only if its market cap was at least $3.0B at the previous day's close. On 321,340,441 shares that's about $9.34 a share. It must also keep at least $150M of liquidity (cash plus unused credit) at every quarter end. So the line goes away in exactly the kind of selloff where it would matter. We never counted it in runway. Source: Core Scientific 8-K, Aug 25, 2026; share count from the 10-Q cover

Counted the standard way, the warrants and the 2029 notes add 29.9% to the share count (417.5M shares instead of 321.3M). The most it could be, if every one became a new share, is about +45%. On that diluted count, Core Scientific's enterprise value (stock-market value plus debt minus cash) is $7.1M per MW of power, not $6.3M, so it's no longer cheaper than Applied Digital ($6.5M per MW). The math is under "Show the math" below.

Paper trigger, not a position. No short. All three gates are required:

Show the math: dilution and exit rules

Dilution, counted the standard way (our arithmetic; inputs from the 10-Q): the warrants use the treasury method, which assumes the company uses the cash holders pay to exercise to buy back shares at the market price. At $13.48 that's 96.4M × (1 − $6.81 ÷ $13.48) ≈ 47.7M net new shares, plus about 6.6M from the $0.01 warrants. Gross, with no buyback: 96.4M + 6.6M + 41.8M = 144.8M new shares, about +45%.

It's $6.8M/MW at the $12.85 cap and $7.9M/MW at the $15.41 target, still below TeraWulf ($10.6M) and Cipher Digital ($13.9M) on our basic-share comparison, so the target is a return to the Oct 9 level, not a re-rating past peers. On the diluted basis Core Scientific isn't cheaper than Applied Digital ($6.5M/MW, basic share count; Applied Digital's own dilution not recomputed).

AMD's warrant (up to 30M shares at $23.47, earned in stages, through Jul 27, 2031) is far above the $15.41 target, so it doesn't cap this trade. 8-K, Jul 27

11. Can Anthropic pay for its computing at a $1.5 trillion IPO?

Our view: yes, if its commitments are spread evenly. The risk is spending piled up early, above roughly $100B a year in 2027–28.

  • The reported commitments are at least $518B over about 10 years. The reported pieces: Google $111.1B, Amazon $110B, Microsoft $31.4B, Broadcom-related leases $161.2B, xAI up to $84.5B, and AMD over $20B. No year-by-year schedule is public. Source: Reuters, Sep 29; xAI terms from the SpaceX prospectus
  • Money raised: $30B at a $380B valuation, then $65B at $965B. Sources: Anthropic, Anthropic
  • Q3 revenue was reportedly $16.8B. Sources: FT, NYT
  • Our math: selling 5%, 10% or 15% of a $1.5T company raises $75B, $150B or $225B. Spread evenly, the commitments are about $51.8B a year, and the gaps in 2026 and 2027 are covered even at the smallest sale.

Who controls the financing (opinion, our analogy): in the spirit of Jay Gould, the 19th-century American railroad financier, the strongest position is controlling the financing around a network, not owning one piece of it. Here, IPO money and the big cloud companies' own investments in the labs flow straight back to Google, Amazon and Microsoft as computing spend. They're investor and toll collector at once. Listed landlords like TeraWulf (about 401 MW leased to Anthropic) get paid only once a lease starts.
