Healthcare Abundance: Consumerization of Precision Medicine

The scarce thing in medicine is moving. For decades the bottleneck was inventing a treatment that worked and then pushing it through a slow, expensive system. AI and genomics are making new therapies more common and cheaper to find. When that happens, the hard problem is no longer "another molecule." It is finding the right person, diagnosing them early, choosing the treatment that fits, delivering it conveniently, and keeping them on it. That is the interface between a human and the healthcare system. This note is about owning that interface — not necessarily owning the drug.

Call the idea by its narrow name: consumerization of precision medicine. Three things meet. First, more therapies exist, so abundance replaces scarcity at the discovery step. Second, people start managing health as a habit — testing, monitoring, refilling — instead of showing up only when they are already sick. Third, digital rails make care something you can buy like a consumer service: a relationship, a subscription, a same-day fill, a wearable that talks back. The winner does not have to manufacture the breakthrough. They may own the last mile of trust, data, and fulfillment.

This is the next layer after a genomics/discovery basket, not a copy of it. Discovery answers whether we will invent more. This thesis asks who captures value when therapies are plentiful and consumers actually show up. Both can win in a growing pie. They are sequential, not substitutes.

The prototype is a direct-to-consumer care company that sells a relationship, not a single formulation. Hims & Hers is the live demo: subscription care, AI inside the patient–clinician loop, and a shift toward an assortment of approved drugs rather than one house product. The business is "we own the consumer around healthcare," not "we sell this pill." LifeMD is the closest analog if you want a second name in the same layer. The metric that matters is not visits. It is revenue per patient times retention times conditions per patient.

Around that relationship sits friction removal. GoodRx does not diagnose. It makes getting a filled prescription cheaper and easier. If the number of possible therapies explodes, price comparison, manufacturer-direct programs, and fulfillment get more valuable. HIMS monetizes the patient. GoodRx monetizes the transaction. Different seats, same flywheel.

Scale platforms can stack the same loop without being healthtech pure plays. Amazon can run Health AI, clinics, pharmacy, and delivery inside a membership. It does not need every step to be a standalone profit center; healthcare can deepen the rest of the consumer ecosystem. CVS is the traditional vertical version: visit, script, insurance, pickup under one roof. The open question for CVS is whether that footprint is an advantage or a cost structure as care moves online. Neither name is in this five-stock trade card, because too much of each company is not this thesis. They stay on the map.

Measurement is the quiet hinge. If hundreds of therapies exist, you need to know who needs which one, when, and whether it is working. Quest's consumer labs and biological-age style loops are "measure, understand, act, measure again." Abbott's Lingo takes continuous glucose from "you have diabetes" to "you care about your metabolism." Wearables and consumer diagnostics turn the body into a feed. That feed is what lets an interface company personalize and retain.

Precision diagnostics sit one layer deeper: who is the right patient, early. Tempus combines tests, longitudinal data, and AI so diagnosis and treatment selection get better with volume. Guardant's liquid biopsy is find, select, monitor, especially in cancer. Natera's residual-disease monitoring is medicine as a feedback system, not a one-time event. These names are the picks-and-shovels of "find the patient." Some also live in discovery baskets. The center of gravity here is still the interface: data that activates a person into care.

Upstream of all this, computational discovery and trial infrastructure still matter. Recursion and Schrödinger help more candidates exist. Medpace, IQVIA, and Charles River convert science into something that can be prescribed. McKesson, Cencora, and Cardinal move volume when more people get treated more often. Those are durable, dull, and not this card. They belong in a fifteen-name scorecard, not in a five-name expression of the bottleneck move.

No public ETF is this thesis. ARKG is discovery. Tema's healthcare-AI fund leans discovery and diagnostics. Global X HealthTech is the least-bad wrapper — consumer care plus devices plus some AI names — but it is not a Healthcare Abundance Index. A custom five-name equal-weight sleeve is the honest first expression: relationship, access, diagnose/fit, find/monitor, continuous measurement.

The five names on this card are therefore HIMS, GDRX, TEM, GH, and ABT. Equal dollars. Timestamped. The basket is a research object with a kill-shot, not a promise: watch whether a consumer platform can own diagnose, fit, fulfill, and adhere at franchise-like unit economics. Until that print is clear, this is a map of where the bottleneck moved — and a small, dated way to keep score against QQQ and Bitcoin.
