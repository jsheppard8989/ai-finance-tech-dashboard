GPU Rental Prices and the AI Builders

Supply and demand for AI compute, what chips earn, how long they last, and how the builders' stocks have traded. Jared Sheppard, AI and Finance.

The short version

AI chips (GPUs, or graphics processing units) rent by the hour at prices published daily. This note covers what drives those prices and what they mean for the companies that build and rent out AI data centers.

  • Demand is growing faster than supply. Google's monthly token count rose about 330-fold in two years. Chip packaging, memory and grid power are growing far more slowly (chart 5).
  • Rental prices are rising again. The H100's rent-now (spot) price fell 69% from October 2023 to January 2026, then rose 24%. The B200 is up 89% from its March low (chart 2).
  • Builder stocks have not tracked rental prices week to week over the past 16 months (chart 7).
  • The accounting assumes chips earn for five to six years. If they earn for less, losses widen quickly (chart 8).
  • The one-year payback claim came from SpaceX's finance chief, not Musk. It holds only at today's short-term prices.

Jared's view (his, not a sourced forecast): compute demand outruns supply until about the second half of 2028. Specialist GPU clouds ("neoclouds") are at a disadvantage because much of their capacity is locked into fixed-price contracts, while SpaceX profits by selling compute at spot prices. For every builder, the key balance is profitability versus running out of funding.

Two growth curves: demand and supply

AI spending now moves the whole economy. The St. Louis Fed estimates AI-related investment added 0.97 percentage points to US GDP growth in the first three quarters of 2025, more than comparable investment added at the 2000 dot-com peak (0.81).

Demand.

  • Tokens (the word pieces AI models read and write): Google processed 9.7 trillion a month in May 2024, about 480 trillion in May 2025 and more than 3.2 quadrillion in May 2026.
  • Capital spending: Alphabet's quarterly purchases of property and equipment rose from $23.95 billion to $44.92 billion over the four quarters to June 2026. SpaceX spent $19.6 billion in its third quarter and guides $35–40 billion for the fourth.

Supply.

  • Chip packaging: TSMC's advanced packaging (CoWoS), a step nearly all leading AI chips pass through, was about 70,000 wafers a month at end-2025. Morgan Stanley projects about 130,000 by end-2026 and 200,000 by end-2027 (reported).
  • Memory: SK hynix says high-bandwidth memory (HBM, the fast memory stacked beside AI chips) has been sold out since 2023 and expects a shortage "even in 2027" (reported). Micron says its HBM supply is fully booked through 2027 (reported).
  • Power: ERCOT, the Texas grid operator, is tracking "approximately 410 GW of Large Loads seeking interconnection" (a gigawatt, GW, is a billion watts), about 87% of them data centers. It has paused connecting new large data centers pending a report due December 10, 2026.
  • Switching on: Musk wrote on August 29, 2026: "Consensus estimate is that ~15GW of AI compute produced in 2027 cannot be turned on in 2027."

On a log scale, demand climbs far more steeply than packaging capacity. Money can speed up memory, transformers and grid connections, but each still takes years.

What the forward market says

A forward price is agreed today for capacity delivered later; spot is the price to rent right now. Kalshi-implied forwards for August 2027, built from few trades so far, sit at $5.50 for the B200, $1.35 for the H100 and $1.16 for the A100. Against SemiAnalysis spot on October 22, that is 59.7% lower for the H100 and 20.1% lower for the B200 (chart 3). This is the main market evidence that new supply will push prices down in 2027. Jared's view differs: he expects demand to stay ahead until about the second half of 2028.

There is no official GPU rental price, and the two private indexes we track disagree. Ornn averages executed rentals; SemiAnalysis blends quotes. On October 13, 21 and 22, Ornn had the B200 well above SemiAnalysis, the H100 slightly below and the A100 well below (chart 4). Ornn's affiliates trade the capacity it prices, which Ornn itself calls "a structural conflict."

Three years of GPU rental prices: has this happened before?

SemiAnalysis's public daily index starts in June 2023 (chart 2).

  • H100: spot peaked at $8.81 an hour on October 15, 2023, fell 69% to $2.70 on January 28, 2026, and was $3.35 on October 24, 2026. One-year contract prices (the middle half of quotes) ran $2.70–3.40 in the first half of 2023, fell to $1.45–1.95 by October 2025 and were back to $2.40–3.20 in July–August 2026.
  • A100 (in full production since May 2020): $1.69 in June 2023, a low of $1.25 in June 2025, and $1.72 on October 24, 2026.
  • B200: $4.93 in June 2025, a low of $3.68 on March 17, 2026, and $6.96 on October 24, 2026.

The normal pattern is a fast loss of value. Epoch AI estimates the computing power a dollar buys in new AI chips grew about 49% a year from 2023 to 2025; its earlier work found GPU price-performance doubling about every 2.5 years. In June 2025, AWS cut on-demand prices for its A100 instances by 33% and its H100 instances by 44%. In November 2018, Nvidia blamed weak results on "excess channel inventory post the crypto-currency boom."

Has this happened before? Not that we could find. An A100 renting six years after full production for slightly more than its first reading in our data is unusual, and we found no earlier data-center chip that held its rental price this long. Our view: this reflects the shortage, not slower aging, and it would reverse if supply caught up.

Musk said on SpaceX's October 20 call that "even old computers like H100s" are "renting for more than they rented when they were new." The data does not support that on spot, which is 62% below its October 2023 peak. One-year contracts are back near their 2023 range, not above it.

GPU depreciation: how long do the books say chips last?

Depreciation spreads a machine's cost over its expected useful life; a longer life means a smaller yearly charge and higher reported profit. Most large buyers lengthened server lives; Amazon has started shortening some.

What if chips earn for less time? Our math, using CoreWeave's second quarter of 2026: revenue was $2,575 million, depreciation and amortization $1,393 million (54% of revenue) and the operating loss $49 million. Spread over 5 years instead of 6, that charge rises about $279 million a quarter; over 4 years, about $697 million; over 3 years, about $1,393 million. The operating loss would grow to about $328 million, $746 million or $1,442 million (chart 8).

The evidence on real life is mixed. The A100 still earns six years in, which supports a six-year schedule. But the H100's rental value fell 69% in just over two years: a chip can keep working and still earn far less than its schedule assumes. Our view: a six-year life is defensible only while the shortage lasts.

Does AI compute pay for itself in a year?

Who said it. SpaceX finance chief Bret Johnsen, not Musk. On the August 4, 2026 call: "Specifically on the AI compute side, we're able to deploy capital in such a way that we're getting less than a one-year payback." On October 20: "At current pricing, new compute deployments are generating a payback of less than a year." Musk added: "It's not impossible that we will have a six-month payback period for compute next year." He said SpaceX rents at "effectively the spot market rate as opposed to sign these long-term deals."

The math. Payback is the time rental profit takes to recover the purchase price. Our calculation for one H100 rented every hour of the year: Epoch AI puts its price at about $30,000 in 2023; at its 700-watt maximum and the August 2026 US industrial average of 9.64¢ per kWh, power costs about $590 a year.

This counts the chip only. Servers, networking, buildings, cooling and staff add a lot, so real payback is longer. Nebius gives a fuller view: its second-quarter 2026 term deals, at $20–25 million a year per megawatt, have an expected payback of "1 year and 10 months," down from two to three years. It sees short-term deals at $40–50 million per megawatt. Our rough math: if full cost is about 1.8 years of term revenue ($37–46 million per megawatt), short-term pricing pays back in about 0.75–1.15 years.

Verdict (our view): plausible, under two conditions: chips rent near spot prices, and they are almost fully used. At the fixed contract prices many neoclouds signed in 2025, payback runs two years or more. Musk's "$30 to $50" per watt figure, which he called "just a guess," gives no time period, so we could not test it.

Rental prices and builder stocks

Chart 7 indexes H100 and B200 spot prices and six builder stocks to 100 from the week of June 9, 2025, when B200 data begins. Rents rose 17% (H100) and 33% (B200). The stocks scattered: Nebius +237%, IREN +153%, Cipher +113%, Applied Digital +63%, Core Scientific +10% and CoreWeave −63%.

Week to week, the link is weak and slightly negative. Over 72 weeks, the correlation (from −1 to +1, how closely two series move together) between weekly changes in B200 rent and each stock ran from −0.15 to −0.22; for the H100, from −0.02 to −0.20. The past month shows the same split (chart 1): from September 21 to October 22, B200 rent rose 22% while CoreWeave fell 38%, Nebius 34% and Nvidia 9%.

Our read: rental prices set what a GPU can earn, but builder stocks have traded on funding, contract news and how much capacity is already sold at fixed prices. Nebius, which reported more than 30% higher prices on older GPUs from the first quarter to the second, rose most. CoreWeave, whose $103.7 billion of contracted future revenue at June 30 is largely fixed-price, fell most.

Who gains if the shortage lasts

Jared's view: the winners in a long shortage are sellers who can reprice often. SpaceX says its short-term contracts are "renewing at higher dollars per watt, not lower." Neoclouds that signed multi-year fixed contracts earn those prices until renewal. For each builder, the question is whether it reaches profitability before funding runs out.

Funding (our math from company filings): months of unrestricted cash at last quarter's pace are CoreWeave 2.9 (4.8 after its September convertible notes, with about $4.4 billion of debt due in the rest of 2026), Cipher 3.8 (7.2 after its October notes), Applied Digital 4.4, Nebius 7.1, Core Scientific 9.1 and IREN 9.4.

How big could demand get? Musk's targets, used only to show scale: "50 million in units of H100 equivalent-AI compute ... within 5 years" (July 22, 2025; about 35 GW at H100-level power, per Tom's Hardware, reported), and "I would be surprised if we brought on less than 13 gigawatts of compute by the end of next year" (October 20, 2026). Set against that, CoreWeave (about 4.2 GW), Applied Digital (about 1.41 GW) and Core Scientific (about 1.1 GW) have about 6.7 GW of power under contract, or about 5.7 GW after removing power the latter two lease to CoreWeave.

What would change this view

  • Rental prices fall through 2027 as new capacity switches on, in line with the forwards.
  • Memory, packaging or power limits clear faster than expected.
  • More companies shorten hardware lives in their filings, as Amazon did for some servers.
  • A builder runs out of funding before it turns profitable.

Limits

  • Vendor indexes. Both rental indexes are unverified commercial products. The forwards settle on Ornn's index and rest on few trades. SemiAnalysis's public data covers the A100, H100 and B200; we found no public H200 rental history.
  • Correlation is not cause. The 72-week correlations are not adjusted for broad market moves, and the September–October split is one month.
  • Our math is simplified. Payback counts chip and power only, at full use. The depreciation recalculation treats all of CoreWeave's charge as six-year assets, though not all of it is GPUs. The Nebius per-megawatt cost is inferred from its stated payback.
  • Musk's targets are xAI/SpaceX build plans. SpaceX quotes come from call transcripts, not audio.
  • Supply figures for packaging and memory are reported via secondary outlets.
