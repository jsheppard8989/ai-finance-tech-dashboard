The Price of Thinking

Why GPU rental rates are the signal to watch in the AI era. Jared Sheppard, AI and Finance.

The short version

Every era has one price that shows where things are heading. The industrial age had oil. Global trade had the cost of hiring a cargo ship. Our view: for the AI era, that price is the hourly rent on a top-end AI chip (a GPU, or graphics processing unit).

GPU rental prices are quoted on Bloomberg and move by the hour. Today they show the newest chips are scarce and the oldest are already getting cheaper. The forward market says the previous generation is next.

We expect a wave of new data centers. So the big question for AI investment over the next two years isn't whether AI software will succeed. It's what happens to the price of compute if the capacity ordered during the shortage switches on in a rush.

We call that risk the Data Center Overhang. We call the reason it's easy to miss the Supply Lag Paradox. Our framework for who wins is Compute Velocity. All three are our own terms.

AI spending is now big enough to move the whole economy

This is no longer just a tech story.

  • The St. Louis Fed estimates that AI-related investment added 0.97 percentage points to US GDP growth in the first three quarters of 2025. At the 2000 dot-com peak, comparable investment added 0.81 points.
  • Fed economists, working from project-level data, project data-center investment at about $370 billion a year by mid-2026. For 2027 their range runs from $360 billion to $930 billion. Even the people who measure the economy can't pin down next year's spending.
  • Meta reported $34.91 billion of capital spending for the third quarter, including finance-lease payments, in its October 21 earnings release. It narrowed its 2026 range to $135–145 billion, from $130–145 billion. It gave no 2027 figure and said it expects "to continue investing aggressively."

The official dashboard still has no reading for the price of what is being built. The producer price index tracks generic cloud servers. A July 2026 Fed staff note tracks chip cost per unit of computing power, memory export prices and capital spending, but no GPU rental rates. The Bureau of Economic Analysis says: "there is not a line item in the U.S. national accounts that can be used to identify and measure the economic impact of artificial intelligence."

Our view: that missing number is the gauge to watch. Spending figures arrive every quarter and look backward. Rental prices move every hour and look forward.

Two exponential curves: demand is exploding, supply is rationed

Shipping learned the hard way in 2008 that ships ordered at the peak keep arriving, and the freight index did not hit "its bottom value of 647 points" until February 2012 (UNCTAD). Our view: AI has a different setup. Demand is compounding, and supply is held back by bottlenecks that money can speed up but not skip.

People struggle to picture exponentials, so look at the numbers.

Demand. - Tokens. Google says the tokens it processes each month across its products went from 9.7 trillion in May 2024 to about 480 trillion in May 2025 to more than 3.2 quadrillion in May 2026. That is roughly 50x, then 7x, in two years. - Capital spending. Alphabet's quarterly purchases of property and equipment went from $23.95 billion to $44.92 billion over the four quarters to June 2026, per its earnings release. SpaceX spent $19.6 billion in its third quarter and guides $35–40 billion for the fourth, per the call transcript (Motley Fool transcript).

Supply. - Chip packaging. TSMC's advanced packaging (CoWoS), the step nearly all leading AI chips pass through, was about 70,000 wafers a month at end-2025. Morgan Stanley projects about 130,000 by end-2026, per its September 2026 update, and 200,000 by end-2027 (reported by secondary outlets; the June note said 120,000 for 2026). Fast, but linear next to demand. - Memory. SK hynix says high-bandwidth memory has been sold out since 2023 and expects a shortage "even in 2027" (reported). Micron says its HBM3E and HBM4 supply is fully booked through 2027; management says demand is "well above our ability to supply" and expects tightness "beyond 2027" (reported by Investing.com summary of the FQ3 2026 call). - Power. ERCOT is tracking "approximately 410 GW of Large Loads seeking interconnection," about 87% of them data centers. - Switching on. Elon Musk wrote on August 29, 2026: "Consensus estimate is that ~15GW of AI compute produced in 2027 cannot be turned on in 2027." Transformers, cooling and wiring, not just chips, set the pace.

Our view: this is the other side of the Supply Lag Paradox. The lag that builds an overhang also rations supply while demand compounds. Which side wins in 2027 is the question rental prices will answer first.

The Supply Lag Paradox

The Supply Lag Paradox (our term): when new supply takes years to build, prices peak at the very moment what may become the next glut is being ordered. So a later collapse in rental rates may not mean demand has failed. It may mean years of ordered capacity have arrived at once.

Data centers fit the pattern. They need land, grid connections, power, cooling and chips, and they take years to finish. Our view is that much of the capacity coming online in 2027 was committed during the 2025–26 shortage.

The shortage was real. SemiAnalysis's one-year H100 contract prices (the middle half of quotes) rose from $1.45–$1.95 per GPU-hour in October 2025 to $2.00–$2.70 in March 2026. Our view: that is exactly the kind of price signal that sets off a building wave.

The Data Center Overhang

The Data Center Overhang (our term): the data-center capacity ordered during the shortage that hasn't switched on yet, and the pressure it will put on rental prices when it does.

Tech giants are building now, and the power grid shows the queue:

  • Texas. ERCOT's first batch study covers 362 large-load projects, 204 of them (66.4 GW) counted as base load. ERCOT has paused energizing new large data centers until it files a verification report by December 10, 2026. The study was originally due April 9, 2027, and that date is now uncertain.
  • The counterpoint. PJM, the largest US grid operator, cut its 2027 summer peak forecast by 2.3% from its 2025 forecast, to 160,451 MW. If the wave turns out smaller or slower than the queues suggest, the overhang shrinks with it.

Our view: a pause doesn't cancel demand. It bunches it, so capacity held back now could land together in late 2027. Our forecast: as the facilities committed in 2025–26 come online through 2027, rental prices for the previous generation of chips could fall hard, however well AI software performs.

The forward market already points that way, though there have been few trades so far. (A forward price is agreed today for capacity delivered later; spot is the price to rent right now.) Kalshi-implied forwards for August 2027 sit at $5.50 for the B200, $1.35 for the H100 and $1.16 for the A100. These forwards settle on Ornn's index. Measured against Ornn's published spot, they imply the H100, the workhorse of the last boom, loses more than half its rental value. They imply that even the B200, the chip everyone wants today, falls by about two-fifths. Against SemiAnalysis spot on October 22, the H100 forward is 59.7% lower and the B200 forward 20.1% lower.

Our read: the A100 is the open question. Against SemiAnalysis spot its forward sits about a third lower, but against Ornn's index, which the forwards settle on, it's flat to slightly higher. The older chip may have already had most of its fall.

Fog of war: two indexes, two prices, one edge

There is no official GPU rental price. There are at least three private indexes on Bloomberg screens, and the two we track most closely disagree sharply.

Ornn (Bloomberg ORNNH100, ORNNB200) SemiAnalysis (Bloomberg SAH100SC)
Method Volume-weighted average of executed rentals over a rolling hour Weighted blend of prices from big clouds, specialist GPU clouds, marketplaces and resellers, with big clouds weighted most
Market signal (our read) Reacts to immediate supply and demand shocks Reflects steadier enterprise pricing
The catch Its affiliates trade physical GPU capacity, which Ornn itself calls "a structural conflict" Our view: it may be slow to show sudden spot crashes

Comparing the two on the same days (October 13, 21 and 22):

  • Ornn prices the B200 well above SemiAnalysis.
  • Ornn prices the H100 slightly below SemiAnalysis, and that gap is steady.
  • Ornn prices the A100 well below SemiAnalysis, and that gap is narrowing.

On SemiAnalysis alone, the B200 has risen about 67% since April 1, from $4.12 to $6.88 on October 22. Its all-time low was $3.68 on March 17.

Our view: this disagreement is the opportunity. With no official GPU rental price, Wall Street is working in a fog of war. Whoever models the spread well holds a proprietary early signal for tech stock valuations. That means knowing when the spread widens, on which chip, and which index moves first. We expect a widening spread to signal a scramble for new chips and a softer market for the rest, which is the shift an overhang would bring.

The market is already arguing about this

From September 21 to October 22, SemiAnalysis B200 rental prices rose 22%. Over roughly the same weeks, CoreWeave fell 38%, Nebius 34% and Nvidia 9% (see chart 1).

Our hypothesis: the stocks are pricing next year's capacity wave, not this week's scarcity. The forward curve shows lower rents ahead. Our read is that new capacity is the reason, and that would shrink the rental income behind today's specialist GPU clouds. Nvidia fell least, which fits the same story: it sells the chips whether rents hold or not.

Something similar happened once before (see chart 2). From October 2025 to March 2026, H100 contract prices climbed while the three stocks slid. From late March to mid-April, the stocks rallied hard. In that one episode, prices moved first and stocks second, and our hypothesis says the pattern will repeat. The October split is the live test: if the stocks are right, our hypothesis says rents will turn down to meet them.

The bull case: what if Musk is right?

The strongest argument against an overhang comes from one of the largest users of compute. Elon Musk's own words:

  • Jul 22, 2025: "The @xAI goal is 50 million in units of H100 equivalent-AI compute (but much better power-efficiency) online within 5 years." Tom's Hardware estimates that is about 35 GW at H100-level power (reported).
  • Oct 20, 2026, SpaceX Q3 call (Motley Fool transcript): "I would be surprised if we brought on less than 13 gigawatts of compute by the end of next year."
  • Same call, same transcript: "we could rent out our compute several times over if we said yes to everyone," with short-term contracts "renewing at higher dollars per watt, not lower dollars per watt."

These are xAI/SpaceX build targets. We use them only to show scale, not as orders placed with anyone. Set them against the roughly 6.7 GW of power that three of the five hard-hit builders have under contract: CoreWeave about 4.2 GW (as of August 11, per its September 17 prospectus supplement), Applied Digital about 1.41 GW and Core Scientific about 1.1 GW. That falls to about 5.7 GW if we take out the power Core Scientific (about 0.59 GW) and Applied Digital (about 0.4 GW) lease to CoreWeave, since CoreWeave's figure may already include it. Nebius expects 5 GW of contracted power by year-end, but that is a target, not a contract, so we show it only as a sensitivity: with it, supply would be about 10.7–11.7 GW. IREN states no contracted GW figure. All of this capacity is contracted, not live. CoreWeave has 1.5 GW active.

This table covers only the contracted power of three listed builders. It is not total industry supply, so it can neither confirm nor rule out an overhang.

In the bull case, one company's own build target alone is about double the contracted supply we could find by end-2027; in that scenario renewals would reprice up and the forward curve would prove too low. IREN gains most because its revenue is price times hours on shorter contracts. Nebius gains on short-term deals and renewals. CoreWeave gains more slowly, because much of its $103.7 billion of remaining contracted revenue (RPO) at June 30 is fixed-price (CoreWeave 10-Q for the quarter ended June 30, 2026). In the 25% case, demand fills only about half of contracted supply by end-2027, and there the same features would flip: IREN would be most exposed, CoreWeave and Nebius would be cushioned until renewal and then reprice toward the forwards, and the landlords Applied Digital and Core Scientific would carry the risk of their largest tenant, CoreWeave. But by around 2030, even the 25% case passes contracted supply, so the team's view is that any glut there depends on targets like Nebius's 5 GW becoming contracts. Our view: if an overhang arrives, renewal timing, not today's price, decides who survives it.

Compute Velocity: our framework for who wins

Compute Velocity (our framework): how fast a company can rent compute, put it to productive use, and release or recycle it when it's no longer needed.

  • High velocity: a company that rents on short terms, runs work efficiently and can shift quickly to cheaper or older chips. It wins when prices fall.
  • Low velocity: a company locked into long contracts at peak prices, tied to one chip generation, or holding hardware it can't redeploy. It pays for every spike.

Our view: for an AI startup, a rental price spike works like an immediate tax. It raises the cost of every training run and every customer query, and it hits equity value before it shows up in earnings. If an overhang arrives, the roles reverse.

Who collects the toll (our view)

  • Nvidia: the main winner while new chips stay scarce. Its risk is slower orders if an overhang pushes even new-chip prices down.
  • Hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta): big and diversified enough to absorb an overhang. Their risk is capacity bought at peak prices that earns less than planned.
  • Specialist GPU clouds such as CoreWeave and Nebius: the most exposed, because they rent out chips whose forward prices point lower.
  • Power and data-center owners: grid connections and sites hold their value better than the chips inside them.
  • AI application companies: the biggest beneficiaries of an overhang, if they keep their Compute Velocity high.

So what?

  • Investors: track rental prices and the gap between the indexes alongside earnings. Use forwards on older chips to gauge any overhang. Judge companies by Compute Velocity, not chip count.
  • CEOs: avoid long contracts at shortage prices unless the work truly needs them. Keep the ability to move between chip generations. Price your 2027 plans against the forward curve, not today's spot rate.
  • Policymakers: build a public GPU rental price series, so the benchmark for a sector this large isn't left to private vendors with trading arms. Plan for an overhang as a supply event, not a collapse in demand.

What would prove us wrong

  • Rental prices stay high through 2027 while new capacity comes online, which would mean demand absorbed the overhang.
  • A proper statistical test finds that stocks move before rental prices, not after.
  • The indexes prove too easy to move, or too lightly traded, to carry a signal.
  • No link appears between rental prices and inflation, research spending or growth. We have already found no credible link to venture funding.

Limits

  • Short history. The H100 contract survey starts in October 2025, and daily spot data on our chart starts in April 2026.
  • Vendor indexes. Both indexes are commercial products we haven't independently verified. The forwards settle on Ornn's index, and Ornn's affiliates trade the capacity it prices.
  • One episode. The lead-lag pattern was checked by eye and isn't adjusted for chip transitions, memory costs or broad market moves. Next step: a lag test against the semiconductor index and the S&P 500.
  • Few data points. We compared the two indexes on only three days, so the spread shows a level, not a trend. We have no B200 trade volume from Ornn.
  • Other explanations. Financing costs at the specialist clouds, or a broad selloff in AI stocks, could explain the October drop. We have tested neither.
  • Bull-case inputs. Musk's figures are xAI/SpaceX build targets used only to show scale, not demand at these companies. The 50M-to-35 GW conversion is Tom's Hardware's estimate (reported). The roughly 5.7–6.7 GW of supply is contracted, not live. It excludes IREN, which states no contracted GW figure, and counts Nebius's 5 GW year-end target only as a sensitivity. The SpaceX quotes and capex come from the Motley Fool transcript, not the audio, and that transcript is inconsistent elsewhere: its summary box gives 20,000 GB300s a month, its body 200,000.
  • Supply-side figures. CoWoS and HBM figures are reported via secondary outlets, and some pages could not be opened to check. The token and packaging series run on different calendars.
  • Our frameworks. The Supply Lag Paradox, the Data Center Overhang and Compute Velocity are ours. The late-2027 timing and the size of the price fall are our forecast, not established fact.
