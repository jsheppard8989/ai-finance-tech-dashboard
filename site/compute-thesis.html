Who Keeps the Profit from the AI Compute Shortage

Which AI compute builders turn the shortage into profit before supply catches up or their money runs out: a three-part scorecard on contracts, cash and chip life. Jared Sheppard, AI and Finance.

The short version

Rental prices tell you how long the AI compute shortage lasts. Contracts, cash and chip life decide which builders keep the profit before supply catches up or their money runs out.

  • The shortage is still on. The rent-now (spot) price of an H100, a widely used AI chip (GPU), is up 24% since January; the newer B200 is up 89% since its March low (charts 1–2).
  • Relief may come later (our opinion): H100 rents could fall through 2027 as newer Blackwell chips add supply. We'll check that against Ornn's H100 spot index, a daily measure of what it costs to rent the chip right now.
  • Only builders that can reprice keep the upside, and all six specialist GPU cloud ("neocloud") and landlord names have under 10 months of cash at last quarter's pace.
  • A payback under a year needs spot-level rents and high use (our math).

Jared's view (his, not a sourced forecast): compute demand outruns supply until about the second half of 2028. Neoclouds locked into long fixed contracts are at a disadvantage; those still selling new capacity are less so. SpaceX profits by selling compute at spot prices. For every builder, the key balance is profitability versus running out of funding.

What the table says (our view). No builder passes all three tests. On new chips even contract-rate payback lands before mid-2028, so tests 1 and 2 separate them. Nebius comes closest; IREN has the most capacity left to price but must build it on time; CoreWeave fails tests 1 and 2; the landlords miss the upside and depend on CoreWeave.

How long the shortage lasts

  • Demand: Google processed 9.7 trillion tokens (word pieces AI models read and write) a month in May 2024 and over 3.2 quadrillion in May 2026.
  • Power, the tightest limit: ERCOT, the Texas grid, tracks "approximately 410 GW of Large Loads seeking interconnection" (a gigawatt, GW, is a billion watts) and has paused new large data centers pending a December 10 report.
  • Switching on: Musk wrote on August 29: "Consensus estimate is that ~15GW of AI compute produced in 2027 cannot be turned on in 2027."

Rents (SemiAnalysis, chart 2). H100: $8.81 an hour on October 15, 2023, the highest since its H100 data began on October 10, 2023; $2.70 in January 2026; $3.35 on October 24. One-year contracts ran $2.70–3.40 in early 2023, $1.45–1.95 in October 2025 and $2.40–3.20 this summer. A100: $1.69 in June 2023, $1.72 now. B200: $3.68 on March 17, $6.96 now.

Have chips ever held value this long? Not that we could find. Normally GPUs lose rental value fast: Epoch AI estimates compute per dollar has grown about 49% a year since 2023, and AWS cut A100 and H100 prices 33% and 44% in June 2025. This time the A100, six years after full production, rents for slightly more than its first reading. Our view: that is the shortage, not slower aging. Musk's claim that H100s rent "for more than they rented when they were new" fails on spot, 62% below its 2023 high.

Our view (opinion): H100 rents could fall through 2027 as newer Blackwell chips add supply. The measure we'll check it against is Ornn's H100 spot index, a daily measure of what it costs to rent the chip right now (Ornn).

Test 1: Can it reprice near spot?

Only a builder that can raise prices while the shortage lasts keeps the upside. CoreWeave's revenue is almost all take-or-pay (customers pay whether or not they use the capacity), so it reprices only as contracts end or new capacity arrives. SpaceX, at spot, sits at the other end. The landlords' rents are fixed for 10 to 20 years.

Test 2: Months of cash against what's due

Runway is unrestricted cash divided by last quarter's cash use (capital spending minus operating cash flow), with no new money. Cash alone comes first; after closed sales of convertible notes (bonds that can turn into shares) second. Customer prepayments flatter Nebius and IREN. Our view: every builder except SpaceX needs new money within a year, so the winners are those that can keep raising it until their capacity earns.

Test 3: Payback at today's rents vs. chip life

Payback is the time rental profit takes to repay the hardware. Depreciation spreads its cost over an expected useful life; a longer life means higher reported profit.

Who said one year: SpaceX finance chief Bret Johnsen. August 4: "…we're able to deploy capital in such a way that we're getting less than a one-year payback." October 20: "At current pricing, new compute deployments are generating a payback of less than a year." Musk added that a "six-month payback period for compute next year" is "not impossible."

Our math for Blackwell. IREN's Dell order of Nvidia GB300 systems costs about $5.8B for about 200 MW of IT load (a megawatt is a million watts), about $29.0M per MW (8-K, November 3, 2025). We assume 500–700 GPUs per MW (no filing gives a count), 85–100% use and power of about $1.06M per MW a year (9.64¢ per kWh, August 2026 US industrial average, plus 25% for cooling). Hardware only. One order is not an industry cost.

What if chips earn for less time? Our math on CoreWeave's second quarter: revenue $2,575M, depreciation and amortization $1,393M, operating loss $49M. Over 5, 4 or 3 years instead of 6, the loss grows to about $328M, $746M or $1,442M (chart 3); counting only chips (about 83% of equipment), about $280M, $627M or $1,205M.

Verdict (our view): under a year is plausible only at spot-level rents and high use. At contract rents our math gives 1.2–1.5 years, and at 2025 contract prices Nebius put its own payback at two to three years. Musk called $30–50 per watt "just a guess" in August; on October 20 the finance chief said pricing is "tracking slightly above the high end" of that range, with no time period, so it cannot be turned into a payback. The A100 supports a six-year life, but the H100's 69% rent drop shows a working chip can still earn far less than its schedule assumes.

Rental prices and builder stocks

Since June 9, 2025, rents rose 17% (H100) and 33% (B200), while Nebius rose 237%, IREN 153%, Cipher 113%, Applied Digital 63% and Core Scientific 10%, and CoreWeave fell 63%. Weekly changes in B200 rent and each stock had a correlation (−1 to +1) of −0.15 to −0.22. From the week of September 27 to October 22, B200 rent rose 19% while CoreWeave fell 38%, Nebius 34% and Nvidia 9%. Our read: the stocks trade on funding and contracts, which the scorecard measures, not on rents. Musk's target of at least 13 GW by end-2027 alone would be about 1.9–2.3 times the 5.7–6.7 GW that CoreWeave, Applied Digital and Core Scientific have under contract (our math; a target, not a forecast).

What would change this view

  • Spot rents (Ornn, SemiAnalysis) turn down as new capacity switches on.
  • Power, memory or packaging limits clear faster than expected.
  • More companies shorten hardware lives, as Amazon did.
  • A builder runs out of funding, or a large tenant stops paying.

Limits

  • Vendor indexes are unverified commercial products that disagree by chip; we use SemiAnalysis for history. Ornn's affiliates trade the capacity it prices, which Ornn calls "a structural conflict."
  • Our math is simplified: an assumed GPU count per MW; runway at last quarter's pace; correlations not adjusted for market moves.
  • Quotes and targets: SpaceX quotes come from third-party transcripts. Musk's 13 GW is a target. Chart 1's packaging and memory figures are reported secondhand.
